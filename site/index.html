Digital

A digital communication system carries a finite alphabet of symbols across a channel that adds noise to everything it carries. This course is the study of how those symbols are chosen, how they are recovered, and how often the recovery is wrong.

What the course is about

One picture, and the number beside it

The screen beside this shows a bit stream in Manchester code with noise added to it, exactly as a receiver would see it. Nothing on it is decoration. The signal-to-noise ratio in the corner drifts slowly between 7 and 11 dB, the noise drawn on the trace is scaled by that same ratio, and the error rate is computed from it.

Pb = Q(√(2 Eb ⁄ N0))

That formula is where the course arrives. Watch the trace get rough and the error rate gets worse in the same breath, because they are one quantity and not two. The bits along the top are the bits the trace is drawing, not a second random string.

Every figure in the course is drawn from the mathematics beside it, and every number is checked by a program that computes it a second way.

The seven modules

One question, asked seven ways

A transmitter is given a symbol and sends a waveform. A channel adds noise to that waveform. A receiver observes the sum and decides which symbol was sent. Every module is either a way of choosing the waveforms, a way of making the decision, or a way of working out how often the decision is wrong.

  1. 0

    The frame of the course

    What the whole subject asks, why digits are sent at all, and what that costs.

  2. 1

    Sampling, quantization and PCM

    Turning a continuous signal into a finite alphabet, and what the rounding costs.

  3. 2

    Baseband transmission

    Line codes, pulse shapes, intersymbol interference and the eye that shows it.

  4. 3

    Geometric representation

    Signals as points. Once distance means something, the receiver becomes obvious.

  5. 4

    The optimal receiver

    Decision regions, the matched filter, and the union bound on the error probability.

  6. 5

    Modulation methods

    ASK, PSK, FSK and QAM, each measured by the same distance as the last module.

  7. 6

    Information theory

    Entropy, source coding and channel capacity — the limit none of the above passes.
